If you have a VA loan and a lender has offered you a streamline refinance — an
IRRRL, in VA shorthand — there is a test the loan has to pass before it can
close. The costs of doing the refinance have to pay for themselves, out of your
own monthly savings, within 36 months.

Congress wrote that test into law in 2018 after a stretch of years in which VA
borrowers were being refinanced repeatedly, each time paying a fresh round of
closing costs for a rate cut too small to ever earn them back. The 36-month
ceiling is the backstop. It is not lender policy and it is not a guideline a
loan officer can work around.

## The rule in one sentence

Divide the fees and closing costs you are being charged by the amount your
monthly principal-and-interest payment goes down. That is your recoupment
period, in months, and it has to come out at 36 or less.

So if the refinance costs $4,200 and drops your payment by $140 a month, you
recoup in 30 months and the loan qualifies. If the same $4,200 only buys you a
$95 reduction, you are at roughly 44 months and it does not.

The clock starts at the note date on the new loan, and it does not care whether
you paid the costs at the table or rolled them into the balance. Rolling them in
is still paying them — the money just comes out of your equity instead of your
checking account.

## What counts, and what is deliberately left out

This is the part that catches people, because the number a lender puts in the
recoupment box is usually smaller than the number on your Closing Disclosure,
and the difference is not an error.

Three things are excluded from the recoupment calculation by statute:

- **The VA funding fee.** Most borrowers assume this is in there, because it is
  the single largest line item on a lot of IRRRLs. It is not. The funding fee is
  a fee paid under the VA's own loan chapter, and the law carves those out.
- **Property taxes and anything held in escrow.** Your new escrow account has to
  be funded, but that money is yours and you are getting the old account's
  balance back.
- **Prepaid items**, for the same reason — prepaid interest and the first year of
  homeowners insurance are costs of owning the home, not costs of refinancing it.

What is left is the real cost of the transaction: the origination charge, any
discount points, title and settlement fees, recording, appraisal if one is
required, and the lender's own line items.

> Two numbers, two jobs. The recoupment figure tells you whether the loan is
> allowed to close. It does not tell you what the refinance cost you. If you want
> to know when you are genuinely ahead, add the funding fee back in and divide
> again — that answer is always a longer wait, and sometimes a much longer one.

A $3,000 funding fee on a loan saving $140 a month is 21 extra months before you
are actually whole. The VA test says 30 months and passes it. Your own money says
51. Both are true, and only one of them is about you.

## Seasoning: two clocks, and you need both

Before the recoupment test even applies, the loan you are refinancing has to be
old enough. There are two requirements, and they run at the same time — you
have to satisfy **both**, not whichever comes first:

1. **210 days** must have passed since the **first payment due date** on the
   existing loan. Note what that is measured from. Not your closing date, not
   the note date — the date the first payment was due, which is usually the
   first of the month after the month you closed.
2. You must have made **six consecutive monthly payments** on that loan.

On a normal payment schedule the 210-day clock is the one that finishes last, so
six payments alone will not get you there. Paying ahead does not help either;
six payments means six months of payments, not six payments' worth of money.

If you closed on the loan in March and your first payment was due May 1, the
earliest note date on a new IRRRL is around the end of November. A lender
quoting you an October closing is quoting something that cannot happen.

## What to do with this

When you get an IRRRL offer, ask for three numbers: the total closing costs,
the funding fee separately, and the new principal-and-interest payment. Those
three are enough to check the lender's work and to answer the question the rule
does not — whether the refinance is worth doing, rather than merely allowed.

A loan can clear 36 months comfortably and still be a poor trade if you are
planning to sell in two years, or if it restarts a 30-year term you are eleven
years into. The recoupment rule is a floor under the worst offers. It is not a
recommendation.

Borrowing from your own retirement account sounds like the cheapest loan there
is: no credit check, a modest rate, and the interest goes back to you. Some of
that is true. But the cost is not where the rate suggests, and the risk is not
where most people look.

## Whether you can, and how much

Not every plan lends. The IRS: "Retirement plans may offer loans to participants,
but a plan sponsor is not required to include loan provisions in its plan." Your
Summary Plan Description says whether yours does. IRAs cannot lend at all.

The limit is set by law. "The maximum amount a participant may borrow from his
or her plan is 50% of his or her vested account balance or $50,000, whichever is
less." If half your balance is under $10,000, the law lets a plan lend up to
$10,000, but "Plans are not required to include this exception." Loans in the past
year can lower the limit further.

Repayment has rules too. "Generally, the employee must repay a plan loan within
five years and must make payments at least quarterly." There is one exception:
"The law provides an exception to the 5-year requirement if the employee uses the
loan to purchase a primary residence."

## The real cost is missed growth

The interest you pay goes back into your own account, so the rate is not the
cost. The cost is what the borrowed money would have earned had it stayed
invested.

Take $10,000 from a $60,000 balance at a plan rate of 8.5%, repaid over five years:
$205.17 a month, and $2,310 of interest paid in. If the money would have earned
7% a year, your account ends the five years $512 ahead of where it would have been,
because the interest you paid in beat the growth. But you paid that $2,310 out of
your own pocket. Net of the $512, the loan cost $1,798: the growth the money would
have earned while it was out.

That figure moves with the market. In a year of strong returns, money outside the
market misses them; in a year of losses, it misses those too. At a 10% return the
same loan costs about $2,876. At 0% it costs nothing, because you only paid
yourself.

The [401(k) loan calculator](/calculators/401k-loan) works this out on your numbers
and sets it beside other ways to borrow.

## What happens if you leave your job

This is the risk that matters most. "Plan sponsors may require an employee to
repay the full outstanding balance of a loan if he or she terminates employment
or if the plan is terminated." If you cannot, the unpaid balance is treated as a
distribution and taxed. You can avoid that by putting the same amount into an IRA
or other eligible plan "by the due date (including extensions) for filing the
Federal income tax return for the year in which the loan is treated as a
distribution."

If you do not, it is taxed like an early withdrawal. "You may also have to pay an
additional 10% tax on the amount of the taxable distribution" unless you are at
least 59½ or another exception applies. In the example, leaving after a year with
$8,324 still owed, at age 41 with $70,000 of other income, single, in a 5% state:
about $1,831 of federal tax, $416 of state tax and $832 of additional tax, $3,080 in
all. The [early withdrawal calculator](/calculators/early-withdrawal) taxes
distributions the same way.

A job change is not always your choice. A layoff can turn a loan into a tax bill at
the worst moment.

## The double-taxation claim

You will hear that a 401(k) loan is taxed twice. It is partly true, and smaller
than it sounds.

The repayments come from your paycheck after tax; the IRS notes that "Loan
repayments are not plan contributions." For the principal, that is not double
taxation: you received $10,000 untaxed and repay $10,000 of taxed money, which nets
out, just as repaying any loan does.

The interest is different. In a traditional 401(k), the interest you pay in with
after-tax money is taxed again when you withdraw it in retirement. That slice is
taxed twice. On the example's $2,310 of interest at a 22% rate in retirement, it is
about $500 of extra tax, paid when the money is withdrawn. Interest on any other loan is paid with after-tax money
too, and is simply gone. In a Roth 401(k), qualified withdrawals are tax-free, so
the second tax does not apply.

## When another loan is better

Compare the 401(k) loan's real cost with the interest on the alternative.

- **A credit card** at 24% costs about $7,261 in interest on the same $10,000 over
  five years. The 401(k) loan is usually far cheaper.
- **A HELOC** at 8% costs about $2,166, a little more than the 401(k) loan's $1,798
  in the example, but it does not depend on your job. It does put your home up as
  security. See [whether a HELOC pays off debt](/calculators/heloc-debt-payoff).
- **A personal loan** at 12% costs about $3,347. See [whether consolidating debt
  saves money](/calculators/debt-consolidation).

A 401(k) loan suits a short-term need, a secure job, and a plan you are confident
of repaying. It suits less well when your job is uncertain, when the repayments
would push you to stop contributing (the missed contributions and any employer
match can cost more than the loan), or when the money would cover spending that
will recur. If what you have is high-rate debt, the [snowball or avalanche
calculator](/calculators/debt-payoff) may show a way out without borrowing at all.

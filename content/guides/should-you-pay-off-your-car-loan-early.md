Paying off a car loan early is one of the few financial moves with a return you
know in advance. Whether it is the best use of the money depends on three things:
how your loan charges interest, what else the money could do, and whether you can
afford to have it locked up in a car.

## The guaranteed return

On most car loans, each month's interest is charged on the balance you still owe.
The CFPB: "Simple interest – which is far more common - calculates the interest
based on the outstanding balance of the loan either on a daily or monthly basis."
So every extra dollar of principal stops costing interest from the day it lands.
Prepaying earns exactly the loan's rate, with no risk. Car loan interest is usually
not deductible, so that is the after-tax figure too; for tax years 2025 through
2028 there is a narrow exception for new, US-assembled vehicles, and if yours
qualifies the loan costs a little less than its rate.

Take $18,000 at 7.5% with 48 months to go: $435.22 a month, and $2,891 of interest
still to pay. An extra $150 a month clears it in 35 months, 13 sooner, and saves
about $838. Once it is gone, the $435 payment is yours for the months that remain.

## Simple or precomputed?

Some loans work differently. With precomputed interest, the CFPB explains, the
total interest is worked out at the start and spread across the payments, and
"Making extra payments does not reduce the principal amount (or interest) owed."
Paying such a loan off early saves less than the simple-interest arithmetic
suggests. "If you plan to pay your loan off early, you want to make sure lenders
are using a simple interest rate, which is generally more common."

Federal law gives you some protection on a precomputed loan. If you pay it off in
full, "the creditor shall promptly refund any unearned portion of the interest
charge to the consumer." The refund method matters: the Rule of 78s, which treats
more of the interest as earned early, is barred for precomputed loans longer than
61 months made after September 1993, but shorter loans and state rules can differ.

To find out which you have, look at the Truth in Lending disclosure that came with
your contract. For a precomputed loan it must include "a statement indicating
whether or not the consumer is entitled to a rebate of any finance charge if the
obligation is prepaid in full or in part"; for a simple-interest loan, whether a
charge may be imposed for paying early. Penalties are a matter of the paperwork
and the law: "Your contract and state law will determine whether you can pay off
your auto loan early."

## Compare it with what the money could do instead

The loan's rate is the hurdle. Money that could earn more elsewhere, or pay down
something costlier, may do more good there.

- **A costlier debt.** A credit card at 24% beats a 7.5% car loan easily. Put the
  extra on the card first; the [snowball or avalanche
  calculator](/calculators/debt-payoff) orders your debts.
- **A 401(k) match.** An employer match is an immediate return no loan can
  approach. The [match calculator](/calculators/401k-vs-debt-payoff) weighs it
  against paying down debt.
- **Savings or investments.** A savings account paying less than the loan's rate
  loses to prepaying. An investment with a higher expected return may win, but its
  return is not certain and the loan's is. In the example, prepaying leaves about
  $361 more by the original end date than investing at 4.5%; against a steady 10%,
  investing would leave about $329 more, if the 10% arrived.

## Keep an emergency fund first

A dollar paid into a car loan cannot be taken back out. If a repair bill or a lost
paycheck arrives after you have emptied your savings into the loan, you may end up
borrowing again at a worse rate. Build the cushion first; the [emergency fund
calculator](/calculators/emergency-fund) sizes it.

## The underwater years

New cars lose value fastest early on, and a long loan can leave you owing more
than the car is worth. The CFPB notes that a high loan-to-value ratio "means you
will owe more than the car is worth – likely for a long period during the loan."
Paying down faster shortens that stretch, which matters if the car is totaled or
you need to sell. If you bought GAP coverage, which covers that shortfall, the
CFPB adds that "you may be entitled to a refund if you sell, refinance, or prepay
your auto loan." The [car value calculator](/calculators/depreciation) shows when
your balance drops below the car's value.

## Make sure extra payments reach principal

Extra money does not always go where you mean it to. "Interest and fees are
generally paid before your payments go towards your loan's principal." The CFPB's
advice: "You may be able to request that your lender or servicer apply more of
your payment to your loan's principal. Check your loan documents first." Then
"review your monthly statement to confirm how your payment was applied." The same
check applies to [extra mortgage
payments](/guides/do-extra-mortgage-payments-save-money).

## So, should you?

Prepaying a simple-interest car loan makes most sense when the rate is high, the
emergency fund is in place, there is no costlier debt or unclaimed match, and you
value the certainty and the freed-up payment. It makes least sense on a low or 0%
loan, where there is no interest to save, or when it would drain the savings you
need. If your rate is high but cash is tight, [refinancing the
loan](/calculators/auto-loan-refinance) may lower the rate without tying up a
lump sum.

A dealer buying your car has to resell it at a profit, so a trade-in offer is
usually below what a private buyer would pay. That gap is the price of
convenience: one visit, no listing, no strangers test-driving it, and the old car
handled in the same deal as the new one. Whether the gap is worth it depends on its
size, on what a private sale costs you, and, in many states, on sales tax.

## Start with real numbers

Get the trade-in figure from more than one dealer. As the CFPB puts it, "Like
shopping for an auto loan, you can get trade-in estimates from multiple dealerships
and negotiate your trade-in value to get the best price." For the private side, use
what similar cars have actually sold for, not their asking prices, and subtract
what selling costs you: listing fees, detailing, an inspection, title fees, and
your own time if you value it.

## The sales-tax credit

In many states a trade-in reduces the price you pay sales tax on: buy a $30,000 car
with a $15,000 trade-in and you are taxed on $15,000. At 7%, that saves $1,050, money
a private sale does not earn you. Other states tax the full price either way, and
some cap or exclude the credit. Check your state's revenue or motor vehicle
department before you count on it.

The credit can decide the answer. With a $15,000 trade-in offer against $16,000
privately and $300 of selling costs:

- **With the credit**, the trade-in is worth $15,000 plus $1,050 of tax saved,
  $16,050, against $15,700 from the private sale. The trade-in wins by $350; a
  private buyer would need to pay $16,350 just to break even.
- **Without it**, the private sale wins by $700.

your numbers, and shows the private price you would need.

## If you owe more than the car is worth

Negative equity changes the stakes on both paths. Owe $18,000 on that car and the
$15,000 trade-in leaves $3,000 unpaid; the $15,700 private sale leaves $2,300. The
payoff comes out of either sale, so it does not change which path leaves more, but
it does mean finding the shortfall.

At a dealer, it is often rolled into the next loan: "a dealer or lender may offer to
roll the balance of your existing auto loan into a new auto loan, but this will make
your new auto loan more expensive." The CFPB warns that a high loan-to-value ratio
"means you will owe more than the car is worth - likely for a long period during
the loan." If the dealer promises to absorb it, check the paperwork: "make sure it's
not included in your new financing or your final loan contract." Paying the
shortfall in cash, or keeping the car until you owe less, avoids carrying it
forward. The [car value calculator](/calculators/depreciation) shows when your
balance falls below the car's value, and the guide on [how fast a car loses
value](/guides/how-fast-does-a-car-lose-value) explains why.

## Selling privately with a loan still on the car

It is possible, with a few extra steps.

1. **Get the payoff amount.** It "might be different from the outstanding balance
   listed on your statement or coupon book because of the way interest is
   calculated, any outstanding late fees or charges, or for another reason." Ask
   about any prepayment penalty too.
2. **Plan how the lien is cleared.** Your lender holds the title until the loan is
   paid. A common approach is to close the sale at the lender or a bank, so the
   buyer's payment pays off the loan and the lender releases the title. Ask your
   lender how it handles a private sale.
3. **Transfer the title and registration** under your state's rules; your motor
   vehicle department lists what each side signs and files.
4. **Protect the payment.** Take a form of payment you can verify, such as a
   cashier's check confirmed with the issuing bank, before handing over the car or
   title, and meet somewhere public.

If you trade in instead, confirm the old loan really was paid off. The CFPB's
advice: "Wait one week and contact the old lender to check that your previous loan
has been paid off."

## Which to choose

Trading in tends to make sense when the tax credit is large, the gap to a private
price is small, or your time is worth more than the difference. Selling privately
makes sense when the car is in demand, the gap is large, your state gives no credit,
and you are willing to do the work. Either way, settle the next car's price
separately from the trade-in, so a generous trade figure does not hide a high
price; the [car affordability calculator](/calculators/auto-affordability) helps
set that budget.
